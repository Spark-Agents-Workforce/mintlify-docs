**The anti-lesson this kills: "upsells are free money."**

The pitch every upsell app sells you is that upsells are pure upside — bolt one on, revenue goes up, no cost. That is the most expensive belief in this module, because every upsell you add to the cart is a decision you force on a buyer who had *already decided to buy*. Get it right and the cart is a second, softer chance to attach the one-time buyer who slipped past the PDP. Get it wrong and you've inserted friction at the exact moment of highest purchase intent — and friction there doesn't cost you the upsell, it costs you the *order*. Upsells are not free money. They are a trade, and you have to know what you're trading.

## The decision

Three cart moves, each spending from the same **friction budget** — the amount of extra decision-making a cart can absorb before people stop finishing their order. (The in-cart upgrade design, the threshold sizing, and the bump-vs-friction rule are frameworks from building these carts; the data has no in-cart attach row. The lifecycle rows below illustrate the *cost of friction*, not the *lift of a bump*.)

### Move 1 — the in-cart subscription upgrade

This is the highest-value cart move: a one-time buyer sits in the cart, and the line item itself offers the upgrade — "Subscribe & Save 15%" as a control on the product they already chose. Done right, the conversion happens *in place*: no re-add, no navigation, no cart rebuild. The price updates with the one-time price struck through and the saving spelled out, and a delivery-frequency selector appears on the line where the control used to be.

Two ways to build the control:

- **Direct convert** — one tap and the line becomes a subscription instantly, with the reassurance on the control itself: "Subscribe & Save 15% — change or cancel anytime."
- **Popover convert** — the control opens a popover first: the program's benefit bullets, a frequency selector, and a confirm button. Two taps instead of one, but the shopper picks their frequency and reads the perks before converting. (A popover is a large tooltip pinned to the control — not a modal, which is the full-screen version that fades the page out and demands an answer.)

Either way, the upgrade must be reversible from the control it created. The frequency selector on the converted line should open to change the cadence — or undo the whole thing, one click back to one-time. Reversibility is what makes the upgrade safe to say yes to: the shopper can see the exit before they walk in.

Done wrong, the upgrade is a full-screen modal that interrupts the checkout march, or a swap that empties and rebuilds the cart. Some stores run the interrupt modal deliberately, betting attach lift against abandonment; no data in this lesson settles that bet, but the proof below shows what friction at this moment costs.

### Move 2 — threshold offers

"Add \$X for free shipping" / "Spend \$Y, get a gift." These nudge average order value *up* — the opposite of what a discount does to the same order (see [Free gifting](/growth-program/offer/free-gifting)) — and they're most powerful when the threshold is set just above your current AOV, close enough to feel reachable. The failure is a threshold set so high it reads as a tax.

The stacking rule is not "one offer only." A healthy cart can run the subscription upgrade, a threshold bar, and an order bump at once, because each points at a *different decision* — subscribe, spend more, add on. The rule is **one offer per decision, with a clear hierarchy**: the subscription upgrade is the dominant ask; everything else is subordinate. Two offers competing for the same decision cancel each other.

### Move 3 — order bumps vs friction

An order bump is a small, complementary pre-checkout add-on ("add the travel size?"): one tap, and the item joins the order. Two things make a bump pay. It's *complementary* to what's already in the cart — related enough to read as completing the purchase, not starting a second buying decision. And it adds *zero steps*: one tap in the cart on the way to checkout, never a detour. Every irrelevant bump, every extra tap is a withdrawal from a friction budget you cannot see until conversion drops. The discipline: **one relevant bump maximum, and only if it clears the friction budget.** When in doubt, cut it — the order you protect is worth more than the bump you might win.

## The proof (and its honest edge)

The data has no in-cart upgrade attach numbers. What it has is a loud, measured lesson about how much the cart-to-checkout moment is worth — which is exactly why you protect it.

At Brand A, the **abandoned-checkout recovery flow earns the highest revenue-per-recipient of any lifecycle flow in the store — ~\$6.15 per recipient, with its hero message at ~\$9.45** *(platform-attributed, not incrementality-tested)*. Read that backwards: the reason abandoned-checkout recovery is the single most valuable-per-person flow is that the people who reach the cart and *don't* finish are enormously valuable and enormously close. Anything you add to the cart that increases abandonment is destroying the most valuable recovery pool you have. Friction in the cart isn't a lost upsell; it's a lost near-buyer.

Second proof, on the flip side — cart-stage interventions *do* work when they're behavior-triggered and relevant: Brand A's **behavioral cart/checkout SMS automations returned ~337.9x ROI, with returning-customer checkout recovery as high as ~\$19.49 per message** — but at a **higher unsubscribe rate** than any other flow *(attribution only; higher unsub pressure noted in-source)*. That is the whole lesson of this lesson in one row: interventions at the cart moment are high-value *and* high-cost. They work, and they carry friction. You run them anyway — carefully, one at a time, watching the unsub and the abandonment as the price of admission.

Honest edge: these are recovery-flow and SMS rows, not in-cart-bump rows. They prove the cart moment is precious and that friction there is expensive. They do not put a number on any bump's lift — that number doesn't exist.

## The artifact — the cart decision tree

Run every proposed cart element through this before it ships.

```text The decision tree
Start: A one-time item is in the cart. What do you want most?
  → Almost always: Attach it (upgrade to subscription). Everything else is secondary.

The in-cart upgrade (do this first)
  Is the upgrade a control on the line item that converts in place —
  one tap direct, or a popover (perks + frequency) then confirm?
    No  → fix that before adding anything else. This is the highest-value move.
    Yes → after converting: saving shown inline, frequency editable on
          the line, one click back to one-time?
            No → add them.  Yes → ship it. Move on.

Adding a threshold offer?
  Does it compete with the upgrade for the same decision?
    Yes → redesign until the upgrade stays dominant.
    No  → is the threshold set just above current AOV (reachable, not a tax)?
            No → resize it.  Yes → ship it.

Adding an order bump?
  Is it complementary to what's in the cart — completing the purchase,
  not starting a second buying decision?
    No  → cut it. Irrelevant bumps spend the friction budget for nothing.
    Yes → is it one tap to add?
            No → cut it or make it zero-step.
            Yes → one bump maximum. Ship it.

The friction check (run after any change)
  Did cart→checkout conversion or abandonment move the wrong way?
    Yes → the last thing you added is the suspect. Remove it. The order > the upsell.
```
