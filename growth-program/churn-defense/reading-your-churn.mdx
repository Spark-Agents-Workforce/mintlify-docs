Your cancel flow tells you why subscribers say they are leaving. Your subscription data tells you where cancellations concentrate. Read either one alone and you will reach for the wrong fix.

A cancellation reason without context can send you chasing the loudest answer. A churn report without subscriber language can show you the leak without telling you what to investigate. Read them together: the number tells you where to look, and the reason tells you what question to ask next.

## What this lesson gives you

- A practical read of churn by type, lifecycle stage, product, cadence, and reason.
- A way to connect cancellation feedback with subscription behavior without treating either as proof on its own.
- A churn triage map that routes the next investigation to the right Growth Program lesson.

## Start with the event that actually ended

[Churn math](/growth-program/numbers/churn-math) already separates voluntary and involuntary churn. Keep that split intact before you diagnose anything else.

- **Voluntary cancellation:** the subscriber chose to leave through the cancellation flow.
- **Failed-payment cancellation:** payment recovery ended without a successful payment. The subscriber may still want the product.
- **Pause:** the subscription is inactive for a planned period, but it has not ended.
- **Merchant-initiated cancellation:** your team ended the subscription, which may reflect support, inventory, or another operating decision.

These events belong to different owners and different fixes. An active failed payment stays in [Dunning](/growth-program/churn-defense/dunning) while payment recovery is running. If recovery ends and the subscription is automatically cancelled, move it into the separate payment-recovery-ended path in [Reactivating cancelled](/growth-program/winback/recapturing-cancelled). A voluntary cancellation routes into the reason and lifecycle analysis in this lesson. A paused subscription belongs in [Resuming paused](/growth-program/winback/reactivating-paused), not in the cancelled pool.

## Locate the moment

The monthly cancellation total tells you volume. The order count and tenure tell you where the relationship broke.

Read cancellations by the renewal stage at which they happen:

- **Before the first renewal.** Inspect the acquisition promise, subscription terms, welcome sequence, first-order experience, and expectations for using the product.
- **During the first few recurring orders.** Inspect delivery cadence, product use, quantity, education, and whether the subscriber had enough time to experience the intended value.
- **During mature tenure.** Inspect changing needs, product fatigue, unresolved support issues, value, and whether the subscription still fits the subscriber's routine.
- **After a save was accepted.** Inspect whether the offer solved the stated reason or only delayed the cancellation.
- **After payment recovery ended.** Keep this segment separate. The next opportunity is restoring a valid payment method, not persuading someone who chose to leave.

Compare the cancellation rate within each stage, not raw cancellations alone. More subscriptions may reach one stage than another, so the largest count is not automatically the weakest point.

Do not treat any of those moments as the cause by itself. They narrow the investigation. They do not finish it.

## Find where the pattern concentrates

Cut the report only when the cut can change a decision. Useful views include:

- **Product and variant.** Compare each product, flavor, size, or format against the active subscriptions that include it. Your highest-volume product will usually produce the most cancellations even when its rate is healthy.
- **Cadence and plan.** Compare cancellation within each cadence or plan. A delivery rhythm may be faster than subscribers use the product.
- **Cancellation reason.** The selected reason tells you what the subscriber reported in the moment.
- **Acquisition source and offer.** Some subscriber groups may arrive with expectations that do not survive the recurring experience.
- **Season or recurring period.** Travel, routines, weather, gifting periods, or category cycles can create a pattern worth preparing for.
- **Save path.** Compare the reason, offer shown, offer accepted, final outcome, and whether another paid order followed.
- **Support context.** Repeated questions, complaints, and manual subscription changes can explain what the cancellation menu cannot.

Every view needs the right base: cancellations for a product compared with active subscriptions containing that product, or cancellations at an order stage compared with subscriptions that reached that stage. Stop segmenting when the next cut would not change what you do. The goal is not the most detailed report. It is the smallest useful pattern you can investigate.

## Read what subscribers told you

Your reason menu gives you a stable top-level view. Free text and support conversations give you the language underneath it.

Use three layers:

1. **The selected reason.** This is the comparable category you can trend over time.
2. **The subscriber's own words.** Keep optional free text attached to the category instead of flattening it into a new label immediately.
3. **A focused conversation when you need context.** Reach out to a specific affected group when the report leaves an important question unanswered. This is subscriber research, not another attempt to stop the cancellation.

Review the **Other** responses regularly. If the same idea keeps appearing and the existing menu does not represent it, refine the menu. Keep the top-level reasons stable enough to compare over time, but do not protect a taxonomy that no longer describes why subscribers leave.

A cancellation reason is evidence of what the subscriber reported. It is not automatic proof of the root cause. "Too much product" may point to cadence, inconsistent use, quantity, or unclear serving expectations. Put the answer beside behavior before you decide which one it is.

## Turn the pattern into upstream work

The cancel screen is often where an earlier problem finally becomes visible. Use the pattern to find the first surface that could have prevented it.

| Observed pattern | First place to inspect | The question to ask |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cancellations cluster before the first renewal | [The first 60 days](/growth-program/first-60-days/welcome-sequence) | Did the early experience deliver what acquisition and checkout promised? |
| "Too much product" concentrates at one cadence | [Flexibility as retention](/growth-program/account-area/flexibility-as-retention) | Do delivery timing and quantity match how subscribers actually use the product? |
| One product or variant has a higher cancellation rate | Product experience, education, and support | Is the issue fit, format, flavor, use, or an expectation set before purchase? |
| Price and spend reasons rise together | [The offer](/growth-program/offer/offer-architecture) | Is the subscription structured around the right quantity, cadence, and value? |
| The same support issue appears beside cancellations | Account experience and support process | Could the subscriber have resolved the issue before reaching cancel? |
| Saves are accepted, then cancellations follow | [Cancellation flow design](/growth-program/churn-defense/cancellation-flow-design) and [Save offers](/growth-program/churn-defense/save-offers) | Did the offer solve the reason or only move the date? |
| Failed-payment cancellations rise | [Dunning](/growth-program/churn-defense/dunning) | Where is recovery failing: retry, communication, payment update, or support handoff? |
| Cancellations repeat in a seasonal pattern | Upcoming-order messaging and flexibility | Can subscribers adjust the next order before the recurring friction returns? |

Choose one meaningful pattern. Write the working hypothesis. Change the earliest relevant surface, then watch whether the pattern changes in a later completed period.

## Carry the reason into winback

The reason does not stop being useful when the cancellation completes. Pass the cancellation date, final state, reason, written context, product, cadence, tenure, and save offers already shown into [Reactivating cancelled](/growth-program/winback/recapturing-cancelled).

That context protects the subscriber from a generic return campaign. Someone who had too much product should not receive an immediate offer for more of it. Someone who rejected a discount because the product was not working should not see the same discount again. A subscription ended after payment recovery needs a payment-restoration path, not the same message as a voluntary cancellation.

Reading your churn decides what appears to be happening. Winback decides whether the original problem has changed enough to make returning relevant.

## The artifact: the churn action record

Your churn report shows where cancellations concentrate. The churn action record turns one qualified voluntary-cancellation pattern into one owned upstream move and one scheduled recheck. Complete one record at a time.

Start with a completed reporting period and the right comparison group. Use a voluntary-cancellation pattern. Failed-payment cancellations route to [Dunning](/growth-program/churn-defense/dunning). Write what happened before deciding why. A cancellation reason narrows the investigation. It does not prove the cause.

### Your churn action record

| Field | Your entry |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Pattern** | In which completed period did which cancellation reason concentrate in which segment, compared with what? |
| **Deciding signal** | What one accessible behavior or subscriber-language theme points to this move instead of the nearest alternative? |
| **Owned move** | Who will make what one change on which one surface? |
| **Recheck** | In which completed comparable period will you read the same pattern again, and will the result make you keep, revise, or stop the move? |

If you do not have a deciding signal, do not guess. Make the owned move a bounded evidence check for that exact segment, such as reviewing the available free text or related support conversations.

### Example: too much product at the fastest cadence

| Field | Example entry |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Pattern** | "Too much product" cancellations concentrate among early recurring subscribers on the fastest delivery cadence for a daily powder, compared with slower cadences. |
| **Deciding signal** | The same segment often skips or moves the next order before cancelling. Available support notes also mention unopened product accumulating. |
| **Owned move** | The subscription owner tests the next slower default cadence for new subscriptions to that product. |
| **Recheck** | Review the same reason, product, renewal stage, and cadence comparison in the next completed comparable group. Keep, revise, or reverse the default. |

This action changes the subscription configuration. It does not add another save offer.

### Example: not seeing results before the first renewal

| Field | Example entry |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Pattern** | "Not seeing results" cancellations concentrate before the first renewal for one wellness capsule product, compared with the same stage across the rest of the catalog. |
| **Deciding signal** | Free text and support messages repeatedly show uncertainty about when and how to use the product. The first post-purchase message does not answer those questions. |
| **Owned move** | The lifecycle owner adds the missing product-use and expectation guidance to the first post-purchase message for that product. |
| **Recheck** | Review the same product, renewal stage, reason, and support theme in the next completed comparable group. Keep, revise, or stop the message change. |

This action changes lifecycle guidance. It does not change the subscription cadence.

After you complete the record, create the task. Attach the saved report or filter context and the deciding signal. Assign one owner and schedule the recheck before work begins. No record is complete until one person owns one move.
