The offer is built; this chapter is how it is shown and worded on the pages that ask for money — the product page, the landing pages, and the bundle builder. Everything here dies in bad copy, so the chapter runs in two halves: the three surfaces, then the writing laws that govern all of them.

## The three money surfaces

### The product page

The workhorse. The subscription option sits beside one-time purchase with the quantity anchor leading (three-month supply first), the price displayed weekly, and the trial line at the moment the benefit lands — peak interest lives here, and the subscription-billing literature's timing law says the trial ask belongs at peak interest, never before the benefit registers. Copy structure: FAB — features into advantages into benefits — because a product detail page is where *what it is* must become *what it does for me*.

### Landing pages

Where paid and outbound traffic lands, so the one surface built per-audience. Structure: PAS — name the problem, agitate it, land the subscription as the solution — with the trial as the close. One landing page per claim you run traffic against, never a generic one for all of them.

### The bundle builder

The build-a-box flow is the quantity play in native form: the shopper assembles the larger order themselves, which makes the three-month anchor feel like their own idea. Every bundle summary ends at the same subscription ask with the same trial close.

## Every section justifies itself in one sentence

Before a section is designed, write the sentence: "this testimonial block exists to build trust before the pricing." One section, one message. A section whose sentence you cannot write gets cut.

## Every surface answers five questions, in order

The Five-Question Method is the skeleton of any page that asks for money:

<Steps>
  <Step title="What is this?">
    Named in plain words, above the fold.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Why should I care?">
    The benefit, in the buyer's outcome — not your feature list.
  </Step>
  <Step title="How does it work?">
    The mechanics: what arrives, when, what the trial is, what cancelling takes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Who else uses it?">
    Proof — see the testimonial law below.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What do I do next?">
    One action, one button, no rival calls to action.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Three tests before anything ships

The Headline Test: read only the headlines — the value proposition must still cohere, because the scanning majority reads nothing else. The No-Image Test: strip every image and icon — the text alone must still tell the story, because images fail on slow loads and in inboxes. The Grandma Test: someone outside your category must understand every sentence, because comprehension friction is conversion friction.

## Frameworks are mapped to surfaces, not chosen by mood

| Framework | Beats | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PAS | Problem → Agitate → Solution | Landing pages, blog posts, any surface aimed at a specific pain |
| AIDA | Attention → Interest → Desire → Action | The homepage, where traffic is coldest |
| FAB | Features → Advantages → Benefits | Product detail sections |
| BAB | Before → After → Bridge | Testimonial sections, transformation products |
| SLAP | Stop → Look → Act → Purchase | Mobile-first cards and short-form surfaces |
| Four Ps | Picture → Promise → Prove → Push | The homepage hero |

## The verbiage laws

Benefits before features — "keeps you dry in a storm," not "a water-repellent jacket." Buttons say exactly what happens next — "Start my 30-day trial," never "Submit." Concrete numbers over superlatives — "arrives every 4 weeks" beats "fast, flexible delivery," and every number on a surface must be true. One name per thing, sitewide — a program called "the Club" on the homepage and "your subscription" at checkout forces re-orientation and leaks trust at the worst moment. Read it aloud — copy that is awkward spoken is awkward read. And role-play the whole page as one named persona before it ships — the walk-through surfaces the missing step you skipped because you already knew it.

## Build the proof stock — from subscribers, specifically

Collect testimonials systematically rather than waiting for them: a standing ask, post-purchase and mid-tenure, aimed at subscribers and not one-time buyers. A review says the product is good; only a subscriber can say *the program* is good — that the third box still earns its place, that pausing was painless, that cancelling wasn't a trap. That is the proof the five-question skeleton spends at "who else uses it," and in BAB format wherever transformation is the story.
