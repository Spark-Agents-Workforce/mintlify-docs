Every surface in the next two chapters sells the same thing. This chapter builds it. Get the offer wrong and the best product page in commerce is lipstick on a subscription nobody keeps; get it right and every surface downstream inherits the fix.

Nine decisions, one centerpiece.

## The centerpiece: the 30-day trial

  <p className="os-chip m-0">Flagship</p>
  <p className="mt-4 mb-0 text-2xl" style={{ fontFamily: "var(--nh2-f-display)", fontWeight: 500, lineHeight: 1.2 }}>
    Try it for 30 days. Start your trial now.
  </p>
  <p className="os-muted mt-3 mb-0 text-sm leading-6">
    The first subscription month, framed as the trial. Same refund liability you already carry. A fraction of the psychological load.
  </p>
</div>

You almost certainly offer a money-back guarantee already. This play does not add a dollar of liability to it. It renames it — and the rename does the selling.

A guarantee and a trial are the same mechanical promise: if the product disappoints inside the window, the money comes back. But they sit differently in a buyer's head. A guarantee says *we will fix it if this goes wrong* — it plants the going-wrong before the first box ships. A trial says *nothing has happened yet* — no commitment has formed, they are just looking. Same refund math. Lighter yes.

So the subscription offer stops being "subscribe and save 10%, cancel anytime, money-back guarantee" and becomes one line: **try it for 30 days**. The first subscription order is the trial. If they love it, they do nothing, and they are a subscriber. If they don't, they cancel in one click and you refund exactly as you would have anyway.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Of the 773 plays we mined out of operator playbooks, subscription-billing references, and churn analyses, none ships this frame for a physical product. Trials are everywhere in software and apps. In CPG the same machinery sits unused on top of a guarantee nearly every brand already carries. That is the opportunity: a proven frame with no incremental cost, that your competitors are not running.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Reframe the guarantee as a trial on every surface that makes the ask: "Try it for 30 days. Start your trial now." The button starts the subscription; month one is the trial window; cancel-anytime is one click in the portal. Choose your dial: a free first order filters nobody, a discounted first order (the mined playbooks' note: charging a token price beats free, because paying anything filters out non-buyers) qualifies intent, and an opt-in renewal — where the trial ends unless they affirmatively continue — is the most honest version of the frame. Start with the discounted first order.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    You are selling risk, and you already own the risk. A pricing playbook's cross-industry benchmark table puts paid guarantees at +2.4% to +21% of revenue (a \$1,000 table with a \$100 no-watermark guarantee at 45% take adds 4.3%; a \$3,000 training package with a \$2,000 results guarantee adds 21%) — because take rates run far ahead of claim rates. The trial frame collects the same asymmetry in conversion instead of cash: everyone who *almost* subscribed but feared the commitment now has no commitment to fear.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The frame is only legal because the mechanics are honest. Negative-option and auto-renew rules require exactly two things of a trial that converts into a subscription: the price at trial end is disclosed clearly **before** the trial starts, and cancelling is as easy as starting. Put the renewal price on the button's surface, keep cancellation at one click, and the aggressive frame stays clean. Hide either one and this play becomes a regulatory complaint with your brand on it.
</Warning>

## Cut the kit to two or three deliverables, then make them exceptional

The retention playbook that ships this play calls overwhelm the number one reason subscribers churn. The fix: optimize for value per second, not seconds of value — a subscription that does two or three things exceptionally beats one that does seven things adequately. Why: density of value survives the unboxing moment; volume of value becomes the pile of unused product that shames a subscriber into cancelling.

## Offer the bigger supply first, then step down

Operators running quantity anchoring report lifts of 20% and more in cash collected. The CPG cut: wherever size is chosen, offer the three-month supply **first**, and let the hesitant step down to monthly. Why: anchoring high makes the standard option read as a concession — and a real fraction simply takes the larger option. The downsell is the same monthly offer you would have led with; leading with it was the only mistake.

## Quarterly billing, both angles

Two angles on the same interval, from the same pricing playbook. First, the calendar: bill \$300 every 12 weeks and five billing events land inside every 52 weeks — \$1,500 collected against \$1,200 from \$100-monthly. Same product, same subscriber, +25% annualized, because a fifth cycle fits where twelve monthly cycles only make twelve. Second, the ladder: quarterly-prepaid at roughly 8% off is the middle rung between full-prepay discounts and no-discount monthly — the concession that costs you least while still reading as one. Quarterly also means four cancellation decision points a year instead of twelve, but that math belongs to the retention buckets; here it is simply the interval that collects more.

## Display the price weekly, bill every four weeks

Straight from the pricing playbook: show the lowest honest number — per week — and bill every four weeks. Why the split: weekly display minimizes sticker shock at the ask; four-week billing minimizes billing events and the pause requests that true weekly billing generates. The display number and the billed number reconcile on the same surface (the next chapter's craft laws make that mandatory), so the play is a framing, not a trick.

## End prices in .99 — unless you sell luxury

A pair, taught together. The worked example: moving a \$47-a-week price to \$49.99 added \$155.48 per subscriber per year — +6.36% — with no measured change in close rate, because sub-dollar differences sit below the buyer's perception threshold and compound across every billing event. The exception is the point: luxury prices end on round numbers, 0 or 5. A luxury buyer is buying the absence of a bargain; a .99 ending hands them a discount signal that cheapens the good. Know which shelf you are on and pick your ending once, sitewide.

## The cheapest tier is where subscribers go to die

One subscription dataset's churn analysis: the basic tier churned at 13.78% — the worst of every tier — and produced \$52 of lifetime value against \$123 at the top tier. The cheap door attracts the customers least attached to the product. JD's corollary for CPG: **the deeper the intro discount, the more get-it-then-cancel you buy.** A 60%-off first box fills the cohort with people who wanted 60% off a box, not a subscription. The fix is not to kill the entry offer — it is the trial frame above, which discounts *commitment* instead of price, plus an entry tier priced so the people who take it can afford to stay.

## Qualify for retention potential, not purchase intent

One case study in the mined corpus moved first-year customer value from \$5,000 to \$42,000 at the same acquisition cost — by changing who the marketing selected for, not what it spent. Purchase intent asks *will they buy once*; retention potential asks *do they have the traits of the ones who stay*. For a CPG brand: pull your longest-tenured subscribers, find what they share — use case, replenishment rhythm, who the product is really for — and point the copy and the targeting at the lookalikes of those people. Selecting for stayers moves LTV without touching CAC, so the whole gain lands in profit.

## Easy in, one-click out — write it into the terms

From the churn literature's contrarian file: buyers name easy signup plus one-click cancellation as the reason they subscribed at all, and name unclear cancellation as the reason they refused. Cancellation ease is an *acquisition* asset — the refusers you never see in any funnel report are refusing over exit friction. The fix: make cancelling one click, then advertise that fact on the ask itself. "Cancel anytime, one click, no phone calls" is a conversion line, and it is only available to brands for whom it is true. It is also what keeps the trial frame's compliance line honest — the terms and the marketing are the same sentence.
