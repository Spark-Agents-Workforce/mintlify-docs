Grown-ups call it winback. On the track it is the return loop — and the map put it in the middle of the machine, not at the end, on purpose. The return track catches people who jumped out or fell through the crack, loops them around, and merges them back in **before** the growth run. A won-back subscriber is not an ending. They re-enter the journey, and then they get bigger.

These are the cheapest subscribers you will ever acquire: they know the product, they liked it once, and they cost an email instead of an ad auction.

## The figure

Ex-subscribers respond at cold-contact rates, and honest expectations keep the program alive: the email corpus benchmarks winback at a 20-35% open rate, a 3-8% winback rate of lapsed customers, and \$1-\$4 of revenue per recipient. Single digits are winning — a list of a thousand lapsed subscribers is 30 to 80 recovered at near-zero cost, every time the flow runs.

The clock matters more than the copy: purchase readiness is timing-dependent, which is why the corpus runs reactivation *quarterly* — repeated campaigns catch people in the window when they are ready again, and one blast catches only whoever happened to be ready that day.

## Enter by segment, not by blast

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The flow trigger the corpus specs is a segment definition, not a date: has ordered at least once AND no order in X days AND not in this flow within 90 days. The 90-day guard is load-bearing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Set X off your own shipping intervals — a monthly subscriber is lapsed at 60-ish days, a quarterly one is not lapsed until well past 100. Split the entry by exit door: jumped-out leavers enter with their cancellation reason attached (the reason menu from [Jumped out](/growth-program-v3/jumped-out) was built for this moment); crack leavers enter flagged card-failure. And segment by value on entry: the corpus's cut is standard versus high-value — three-plus orders or top-20% spend.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The reason decides the pitch. Too-much-product lapsed wants a gentler cadence or a smaller box, not a discount. Card-failure lapsed never wanted to leave at all — a fix-your-card message with the subscription held open beats any offer. The 90-day guard exists because cycling the same lapsed customer through winback repeatedly trains them to ignore you — or worse, to wait for the discount.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The ladder: content first, discount last

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The winback playbook's flow is exactly three emails — day 0, +14 days, +14 days with a 48-hour close — on a four-rung incentive ladder: nothing, nothing, free shipping or ~10%, then 15-20%. Its stated rationale for rung one: do not train people to expect discounts for leaving.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Email one, no offer: what has changed since they left — new products, the fixes you shipped (if the flow knows their exit reason, this is where you answer it). Email two, still light: social proof and a nudge, free shipping at most. Email three, the real offer with a hard 48-hour expiry — and the expiry is honest, or it teaches the list that your deadlines are theater. Purchase checks between each send exit anyone who came back. For high-value lapsed, the corpus's own numbers justify more: 15-20% or an exclusive bundle, and for the top-10%-spend tier, skip the ladder entirely — a personal founder email, the generous offer, and a support alert for personal outreach.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Leading with discounts poisons two wells at once: it prices returning below staying (your loyal subscribers eventually notice), and it converts your winback list into a coupon-waiting room. Content-first costs one email of patience and protects both. The value split exists because a higher expected LTV justifies a larger recovery spend per head — and the founder email works on the same mechanism as the exit interview: the perceived personal cost of the channel is the message.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Make returning one click

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Every winback email in the corpus's spec ends at an easy reactivation CTA — the flow's conversion surface is the landing, not the copy.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    The button restores the old subscription — product, interval, address — in one click, not a walk through the full signup. The one-click exit that Get in promised has a twin here: one-click return. If they left over too-much-product, the reactivation surface offers the longer interval *before* they have to ask for it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Everything the lapsed subscriber knows about you ends at friction: they remember signup, they remember the portal. A return that takes ten fields is a re-acquisition; a return that takes one click is a resumption. The 3-8% benchmark is earned at the button, not in the subject line.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Know when to stop

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The corpus's terminal branch is explicit: after a failed winback, suppress — do not keep sending. Its sunset trigger: mailed at least 10 times in 90 days with no open or click in 90 days.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Non-responders exit to suppression, not to the newsletter. The winback flow is the last dedicated sequence a lapsed subscriber receives; after it, they hear from you at most quarterly, when the reactivation promotion runs and catches whoever's window has come around.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Dead addresses and dead interest cost deliverability for the whole list — the subscribers you still have pay the price for the ones you keep chasing. The ladder's own final rung says it: do not bribe people to stay on a list they do not read.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The hands in this bucket

The founder email to a top-spend lapsed customer is the hands at their highest leverage — a human noticing a valuable person left, and saying so personally. But most winback conversations start inbound: the returner who writes in asking whether their old account still exists. Patch what broke: restore it on the spot, old perks intact, no forms. More than they asked for: acknowledge the absence — welcome back, here is what is new — instead of pretending the gap never happened. A depersonalized return is a second chance wasted.

---

The border of this bucket is the merge: the moment a returner's next box ships, they are not a winback anymore. They rejoin the track where the return loop merges — before the growth run — because the best thing a recovered subscriber can do is what every kept subscriber does next: [Get bigger](/growth-program-v3/get-bigger).
