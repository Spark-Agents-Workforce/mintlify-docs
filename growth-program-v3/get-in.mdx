Grown-ups call it acquisition. On the track it is the funnel: how people land on the track at all.

This bucket has one law, and it is the sentence at the top of the page: make the first yes easy without poisoning the second yes. Half the plays below make the yes easier. The other half stop you from buying subscribers who were never going to stay. You need both, because a first yes that churns in week three is not acquisition — it is a refund with extra steps.

The page runs in four moves: the flagship play, the offer you carry, the surfaces you carry it to, and how to write every one of those surfaces.

## The flagship play: the 30-day trial

<div className="os-hero-box not-prose my-6 px-6 py-8 sm:px-10">
  <p className="os-chip m-0">Flagship</p>
  <p className="mt-4 mb-0 text-2xl" style={{ fontFamily: "var(--nh2-f-display)", fontWeight: 500, lineHeight: 1.2 }}>
    Try it for 30 days. Start your trial now.
  </p>
  <p className="os-muted mt-3 mb-0 text-sm leading-6">
    The first subscription month, framed as the trial. Same refund liability you already carry. A fraction of the psychological load.
  </p>
</div>

You almost certainly offer a money-back guarantee already. This play does not add a dollar of liability to it. It renames it — and the rename does the selling.

A guarantee and a trial are the same mechanical promise: if the product disappoints inside the window, the money comes back. But they sit differently in a buyer's head. A guarantee says *we will fix it if this goes wrong* — it plants the going-wrong before the first box ships. A trial says *nothing has happened yet* — no commitment has formed, they are just looking. Same refund math. Lighter yes.

So the subscription offer stops being "subscribe and save 10%, cancel anytime, money-back guarantee" and becomes one line: **try it for 30 days**. The first subscription order is the trial. If they love it, they do nothing, and they are a subscriber. If they don't, they cancel in one click and you refund exactly as you would have anyway.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Of the 773 plays we mined out of operator playbooks, subscription-billing references, and churn analyses, none ships this frame for a physical product. Trials are everywhere in software and apps. In CPG the same machinery sits unused on top of a guarantee nearly every brand already carries. That is the opportunity: a proven frame with no incremental cost, that your competitors are not running.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Reframe the guarantee as a trial on every surface that makes the ask: "Try it for 30 days. Start your trial now." The button starts the subscription; month one is the trial window; cancel-anytime is one click in the portal. Choose your dial: a free first order filters nobody, a discounted first order (the mined playbooks' note: charging a token price beats free, because paying anything filters out non-buyers) qualifies intent, and an opt-in renewal — where the trial ends unless they affirmatively continue — is the most honest version of the frame. Start with the discounted first order.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    You are selling risk, and you already own the risk. A pricing playbook's cross-industry benchmark table puts paid guarantees at +2.4% to +21% of revenue (a \$1,000 table with a \$100 no-watermark guarantee at 45% take adds 4.3%; a \$3,000 training package with a \$2,000 results guarantee adds 21%) — because take rates run far ahead of claim rates. The trial frame collects the same asymmetry in conversion instead of cash: everyone who *almost* subscribed but feared the commitment now has no commitment to fear.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
The frame is only legal because the mechanics are honest. Negative-option and auto-renew rules require exactly two things of a trial that converts into a subscription: the price at trial end is disclosed clearly **before** the trial starts, and cancelling is as easy as starting. Put the renewal price on the button's surface, keep cancellation at one click, and the aggressive frame stays clean. Hide either one and this play becomes a regulatory complaint with your brand on it.
</Warning>

Place the trial ask at the moment of peak interest — the subscription-billing literature's timing law. Peak interest on a store is the product page after the benefit has landed, the post-purchase page after the card was already charged, and the pop-up on a returning visitor. It is never the top bar of a first visit.

## The offer you carry to every surface

The surfaces below only work if the thing they sell is right. Eight plays, each in figure-fix-why order, each one offer-level: fix them once and every surface inherits the fix.

### Cut to two or three deliverables, then make them exceptional

The retention playbook that ships this play calls overwhelm the number one reason subscribers churn. The fix: optimize for value per second, not seconds of value — a subscription that does two or three things exceptionally beats one that does seven things adequately. Why: density of value survives the unboxing moment; volume of value becomes the pile of unused product that shames a subscriber into cancelling.

### Offer the bigger supply first, then step down

Operators running quantity anchoring report lifts of 20% and more in cash collected. The CPG cut: on the surfaces where size is chosen, offer the three-month supply **first**, and let the hesitant step down to monthly. Why: anchoring high makes the standard option read as a concession — and a real fraction simply takes the larger option. The downsell is the same monthly offer you would have led with; leading with it was the only mistake.

### Quarterly billing, both angles

Two angles on the same interval, from the same pricing playbook. First, the calendar: bill \$300 every 12 weeks and five billing events land inside every 52 weeks — \$1,500 collected against \$1,200 from \$100-monthly. Same product, same subscriber, +25% annualized, because a fifth cycle fits where twelve monthly cycles only make twelve. Second, the ladder: quarterly-prepaid at roughly 8% off is the middle rung between full-prepay discounts and no-discount monthly — the concession that costs you least while still reading as one. Quarterly also means four cancellation decision points a year instead of twelve, but that math belongs to the retention buckets; here it is simply the interval that collects more.

### Display the price weekly, bill every four weeks

Straight from the pricing playbook: show the lowest honest number — per week — and bill every four weeks. Why the split: weekly display minimizes sticker shock at the ask; four-week billing minimizes billing events and the pause requests that true weekly billing generates. The display number and the billed number reconcile on the same surface (the page-craft section below makes that mandatory), so the play is a framing, not a trick.

### End prices in .99 — unless you sell luxury

A pair, taught together. The worked example: moving a \$47-a-week price to \$49.99 added \$155.48 per subscriber per year — +6.36% — with no measured change in close rate, because sub-dollar differences sit below the buyer's perception threshold and compound across every billing event. The exception is the point: luxury prices end on round numbers, 0 or 5. A luxury buyer is buying the absence of a bargain; a .99 ending hands them a discount signal that cheapens the good. Know which shelf you are on and pick your ending once, sitewide.

### The cheapest tier is where subscribers go to die

One subscription dataset's churn analysis: the basic tier churned at 13.78% — the worst of every tier — and produced \$52 of lifetime value against \$123 at the top tier. The cheap door attracts the customers least attached to the product. JD's corollary for CPG: **the deeper the intro discount, the more get-it-then-cancel you buy.** A 60%-off first box fills the cohort with people who wanted 60% off a box, not a subscription. The fix is not to kill the entry offer — it is the trial frame above, which discounts *commitment* instead of price, plus an entry tier priced so the people who take it can afford to stay.

### Qualify for retention potential, not purchase intent

One case study in the mined corpus moved first-year customer value from \$5,000 to \$42,000 at the same acquisition cost — by changing who the marketing selected for, not what it spent. Purchase intent asks *will they buy once*; retention potential asks *do they have the traits of the ones who stay*. For a CPG brand: pull your longest-tenured subscribers, find what they share — use case, replenishment rhythm, who the product is really for — and point the copy and the targeting at the lookalikes of those people. Selecting for stayers moves LTV without touching CAC, so the whole gain lands in profit.

### Easy in, one-click out — say so out loud

From the churn literature's contrarian file: buyers name easy signup plus one-click cancellation as the reason they subscribed at all, and name unclear cancellation as the reason they refused. Cancellation ease is an *acquisition* asset — the refusers you never see in any funnel report are refusing over exit friction. The fix: make cancelling one click, then advertise that fact on the ask itself. "Cancel anytime, one click, no phone calls" is a conversion line, and it is only available to brands for whom it is true.

## On the store

JD's cut, and the organizing law of this bucket: the store is not a website with a subscribe button somewhere — basically **everything on the store** is a surface for the subscription ask. Nine surfaces. Each one gets the offer above, the trial frame where it fits, and the writing laws from the last section. Your team builds each surface click-by-click; this is the map of what each one is for.

### Landing pages

Where paid and outbound traffic lands, so the one surface built per-audience. Structure: PAS — name the problem, agitate it, land the subscription as the solution — with the trial as the close. One landing page per claim you run traffic against, never a generic one for all of them.

### The product page

The workhorse. The subscription option sits beside one-time purchase with the quantity anchor leading (three-month supply first), the price displayed weekly, and the trial line at the moment the benefit lands — peak interest lives here. Copy structure: FAB — features into advantages into benefits — because a product detail page is where *what it is* must become *what it does for me*.

### The collections page

The grid is an ask, not a menu. Subscription-eligible products carry the offer at card level — the weekly price and the trial line — so the shopper meets the subscription before the click, not after. Mobile-first copy here: SLAP — stop, look, act, purchase.

### The bundle builder

The build-a-box flow is the quantity play in native form: the shopper assembles the larger order themselves, which makes the three-month anchor feel like their own idea. Every bundle summary ends at the same subscription ask with the same trial close.

### The top bar

One line, sitewide, persistent. It carries the trial sentence — "Try it for 30 days" — or it carries the one-click-cancel promise. It never carries a naked percentage, because a percentage in the top bar is the deep-intro-discount trap advertising itself.

### The dedicated subscriptions page

The play the corpus taught for apps — promote the subscription *outside* the product so the purchase can start before the product is even in hand — lands on a store as a page: **/subscribe, the subscription as its own product.** Its own URL. Its own benefits section, written about the *program* (the trial, the flexibility, the one-click exit, what arrives when) rather than any single SKU. Its own social proof, from subscribers. This is the ad destination for subscription-intent traffic and the link every outbound message points at. If the subscription only exists as a widget on product pages, it is a payment option. This page is what makes it a product.

### The cart

The switch surface. Every one-time item in the cart carries the switch-to-subscription offer with the delta shown in dollars — what this box costs one-time versus on the program. The cart is the last moment the choice is cheap; after checkout it costs a support ticket.

### Checkout

The switch offer once more, in one line, at the point of no return. Nothing new is introduced at checkout — no new prices, no new names, nothing that forces re-reading (the coherence law below). Just the last quiet chance to take the better deal.

### The post-purchase upgrade

The card was charged; the risk question is answered; this is the cheapest yes on the store. The one-time buyer meets the subscription here — the trial frame, the subscriber benefits, brief — as a one-click upgrade on the order they just placed. Brief is load-bearing: the billing literature's onboarding law is a succinct case and clear terms, because the buyer is done shopping and every extra paragraph costs takers.

## Outbound

Off the store there are five channels. Same offer, same frames, one difference: outbound interrupts, so the ask must arrive inside something worth opening.

### The list

Your email list is one-time buyers and never-bought subscribers-in-waiting, and the job is conversion content: the content that moves a list member to the program. The honest state of the art: the corpus is thin here, and the working angle is the newsletter that treats the subscription as editorial subject — the routine the product lives in, what subscribers know that one-time buyers don't, what arrives this month — with the /subscribe page as every issue's destination. Which content types convert best is an open research lane; what ships today is the angle, not a false benchmark.

### Pop-ups

The pop-up earns its interruption by offering the trial, not a naked discount for an email address. Trial frame, one field, button says what happens next. On a returning visitor — a peak-interest moment — it is the highest-leverage placement the store has.

### Browse abandonment

They looked at the product and left. The follow-up leads with the subscription angle, not the product they already declined: the trial, the one-click exit, the weekly price. They said no to buying a box; nobody asked them about trying a program.

### Come-back-to-store emails

The lapsed one-time buyer gets the program pitch, because "buy another box" is the offer they already stopped taking. The replenishment rhythm they abandoned is the argument: this is the product that runs out — the program exists so it doesn't.

### Blog posts

Long-form PAS. Each post names one problem your buyer actually searches, agitates it honestly, and resolves into the program — and every post ends at /subscribe. A blog post that ends nowhere is content marketing; a post that ends at the subscriptions page is a landing page with patience.

## Write every surface like this

Everything above dies in bad copy. This block is the page-craft law for every on-store surface — and outbound inherits it. It is how a world-class team writes a store, taught as rules you can run.

**Every section justifies itself in one sentence.** Before a section is designed, write the sentence: "this testimonial block exists to build trust before the pricing." One section, one message. A section whose sentence you cannot write gets cut.

**Every surface answers five questions, in order.** The Five-Question Method is the skeleton of any page that asks for money:

<Steps>
  <Step title="What is this?">
    Named in plain words, above the fold.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Why should I care?">
    The benefit, in the buyer's outcome — not your feature list.
  </Step>
  <Step title="How does it work?">
    The mechanics: what arrives, when, what the trial is, what cancelling takes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Who else uses it?">
    Proof — see the testimonial law below.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What do I do next?">
    One action, one button, no rival calls to action.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**Three tests before anything ships.** The Headline Test: read only the headlines — the value proposition must still cohere, because the scanning majority reads nothing else. The No-Image Test: strip every image and icon — the text alone must still tell the story, because images fail on slow loads and in inboxes. The Grandma Test: someone outside your category must understand every sentence, because comprehension friction is conversion friction.

**Frameworks are mapped to surfaces, not chosen by mood.**

| Framework | Beats | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PAS | Problem → Agitate → Solution | Landing pages, blog posts, any surface aimed at a specific pain |
| AIDA | Attention → Interest → Desire → Action | The homepage, where traffic is coldest |
| FAB | Features → Advantages → Benefits | Product detail sections |
| BAB | Before → After → Bridge | Testimonial sections, transformation products |
| SLAP | Stop → Look → Act → Purchase | Mobile-first cards and short-form surfaces |
| Four Ps | Picture → Promise → Prove → Push | The homepage hero |

**The verbiage laws.** Benefits before features — "keeps you dry in a storm," not "a water-repellent jacket." Buttons say exactly what happens next — "Start my 30-day trial," never "Submit." Concrete numbers over superlatives — "arrives every 4 weeks" beats "fast, flexible delivery," and every number on a surface must be true. One name per thing, sitewide — a program called "the Club" on the homepage and "your subscription" at checkout forces re-orientation and leaks trust at the worst moment. Read it aloud — copy that is awkward spoken is awkward read. And role-play the whole page as one named persona before it ships — the walk-through surfaces the missing step you skipped because you already knew it.

**Build the proof stock — from subscribers, specifically.** Collect testimonials systematically rather than waiting for them: a standing ask, post-purchase and mid-tenure, aimed at subscribers and not one-time buyers. A review says the product is good; only a subscriber can say *the program* is good — that the third box still earns its place, that pausing was painless, that cancelling wasn't a trap. That is the proof the five-question skeleton spends at "who else uses it," on /subscribe, and in BAB format wherever transformation is the story.
