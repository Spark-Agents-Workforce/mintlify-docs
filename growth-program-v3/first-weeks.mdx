Grown-ups call it onboarding, but by now the box is on the counter and the ritual has started — [First order](/growth-program-v3/first-order) did that. This bucket is the fortification program for what comes next: order two onward, through the 60-90 day retention cliff. The operating belief is blunt: get them past 60 or 90 days, and they are retained forever. Everything on this page exists to carry a new subscriber across that line.

Nine stations, in the order you build them: the map of the cliff, the second order, the cadence, the deepening, the early-warning system, the flexibility valves, the mesh, the milestone line, and the commitment pivot that ends the cliff outright.

## The cliff map

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The corpus agrees on where the danger lives: most subscription churn lands in the first 2-3 months — the named early-risk period — and the closest thing to a named program is fortify-the-first-90-days, graded by retention at six months. The curve's shape has a known distortion: expect a shake-the-tree spike of up to +50% churn in month one as ghost subscribers surface and cancel (the playbook's worked trajectory: month one 10% to 15%, month two down to 7%, month three to 3%). Month one looks worse before it gets better; do not panic-patch the spike.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Read the cliff on a cohort table, not an aggregate: each row a start month, each cell the % still active N months later, column one always 100%. Cut it per subscription type — aggregate churn hides which product is bleeding — and segment tenure the corpus's way: New (under 1 month), Returning (under 6), Loyal (over 6). Then place your defenses where your own curve says: analyze when subscribers actually cancel and put a valuable unlock just after each spike — if the cliff is month 3, something unlocks at month 4.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The cheapest-tier warning from [Build the offer](/growth-program-v3/get-in/build-the-offer) compounds here — the basic tier is the churn hotspot (13.78% vs \$123-LTV top tiers), so the cliff is steepest exactly where the intro offer was deepest. The map tells you where to spend; without it every play below is deployed blind.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Info>
**Frontier: the day-numbered program.** The corpus names the 90-day fortification and lists ingredient bundles — but a day-7 / day-21 / day-45 / day-75 touch map exists nowhere in 773 cards, and no benchmark anywhere grades "% surviving to order 3." OpenSubs is writing the day-numbered CPG program first; the stations below are its parts, sequenced. Same honesty on **efficacy expectation-setting**: supplements take 4-8 weeks to show results and the churn lands in exactly that gap — the corpus never once addresses the delayed-benefit window. Both ship as originals when they are real.
</Info>

## The second order

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Order two is the shipment that decides whether there is a third — and the conversion math turns hard in your favor once it lands: repeat customers convert 60-70% better than other shoppers, and a customer with two previous purchases is 9x more likely to convert than a first-timer. Every play here is buying that second data point.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Run order two on the consumption clock. The corpus's replenishment windows by category — coffee 14-21 days, supplements 25-30, pet food 21-30, skincare 30-45 — set the interval; the pre-shipment notice (the corpus's renewal warning: the day before the charge, plan name, exact date) makes the second charge expected instead of discovered. The repeat split from the flow spec now pays: order-two messaging acknowledges the history and skips the brand education. And the replenishment nudge pattern for anyone off-cycle: fire at expected reorder date +14 days, personalized to their product, one-click reorder, **no discount** — the corpus is explicit that the first nudge carries none.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    In CPG the second order is not an email event, it is a replenishment-timing event. A charge that lands while the first tub is still half full reads as a bill; the same charge landing as the scoop hits bottom reads as service. The no-discount rule on the nudge protects the same well as the winback ladder: pay people to come back and they learn to leave.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Info>
**Frontier: consumption pacing.** The single biggest early-tenure failure in supplements and drinks — the subscriber consuming slower than you ship, stockpiling, and cancelling on "I already have three tubs" — has zero cards in the corpus. Reminder timing exists; cadence *correction* does not. The nearest miss files the right lever under pricing (every-other-month as a quantity downsell) and never connects it to early tenure. Detect the oversupply, offer the slower interval before the shelf fills: OpenSubs original, under development. Related and also unwritten anywhere: **sampling and variety as a boredom cure** — flavor fatigue kills food and drink subscriptions by month 3, and the corpus's only touch on it is "swap product" at the cancel screen.
</Info>

## The cadence

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The retention playbook's floor: touch every customer 1-on-1 every 2-3 weeks minimum — twice a week for at-risk. Against that floor, two ceilings: match cadence to the segment's observed purchase frequency (a quarterly buyer does not want weekly email), and do not overload — the corpus states both the drumbeat and the noise limit.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    The automated layer keeps the product in consciousness — timely, purposeful, meaningful, nothing else. The personal layer runs the ACA script: acknowledge what you see them doing, compliment something specific, ask a question. Personalize on real account context only — name, milestone, what they bought — because broken personalization ("Hey there, %name!") is worse than none. Channel choice follows the save-rate ladder: call, voice note, text, personal email, automated email, in descending order of effect and cost.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Positive business-to-customer interactions are what stop people wanting to unsubscribe in the first place — prevention upstream of the cancel button beats friction at it. The cadence is that prevention, scheduled.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The deepening

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Depth protects tenure, and the corpus says it from two datasets: higher-spending subscribers churn less, and order frequency predicts churn better than spend. The inverse names the danger: value-for-money is the weakest satisfaction dimension and the one tied to churn — the subscriber who stops feeling the value starts pricing the box.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Keep teaching the product past box one: the routine-builder pattern (usage education keyed to what they bought, short-dated incentive) extends into weeks 2-8 with second-order content — new uses, pairings, the why behind the formula. Fight feature blindness the corpus's way: find which behaviors correlate with long-term retention in your own cohort data, then push those behaviors back into attention; re-release what subscribers have stopped noticing. Grade all of it on the 30-day activation scoreboard carried forward from First order.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    A subscription survives on the size of its footprint in the subscriber's week. Every additional use, ritual, and unlocked capability is surface area the cancel decision has to tear through.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The early-warning system

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The largest cluster in the whole corpus — 36 cards — says one thing: usage churn precedes billing churn, so intercept when usage drops, not when the cancel arrives. The decay is legible week by week (the corpus's worked pattern: three sessions week one, two in week two — the warning — one by week four, cancel by week five; intervening at the week-two dip returns the customer to full engagement by week three). And the complaint multiplier runs hottest here: a low-tenure customer with an active complaint is 3-4x more likely to churn — intervene before day 30.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    For CPG the corpus gives the exact translation: healthy = regular use and on-time reorder; warning = a delayed or skipped reorder; trigger = an incentive email to resume. Derive thresholds from your own reorder cycle — 1.5x for At Risk, 2.5x for Lapsed — never generic day-counts. Wire the six named warning signals: engagement drop, support ticket, downgrade, sub-3-star review, skip/pause, expected-reorder-date-passed. Then tier the response like the corpus's risk buckets: high risk gets proactive human outreach within 24 hours; medium gets a nudge and a one-question pulse; low gets reviews, referrals, and left alone. Match intervention cost to risk — expensive saves only above high probability, and gate the priciest on high-value too.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Two corrections keep the system honest. Recency misleads — in one dataset churners had *shorter* median days-since-last-order than non-churners (~2 vs ~4 days; a flurry can be a goodbye) — so watch frequency trend, not last-seen. And when the cause is a service failure, marketing email is the wrong instrument entirely: concierge recovery, a human fixing the thing, is the play — one bad support interaction can end a decade-long subscription, and the qualitative signal from support is the input analytics cannot supply.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The flexibility valves

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The mechanics all exist in the platform layer: skip-next-cycle consumes one billing period and self-clears; pause keeps the billing anchor alive and blocks execution; downgrade defers to the next renewal. And the accounting is ruled: a pause counts as a retention win, not as churn.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Hand the valves over in weeks 2-8, prominently, in the portal and in the cadence emails — skip, pause, swap, slow down, smaller — before anyone is looking for the exit. The corpus's quantity-downsell menu is the size ladder (fewer units, less often, smaller). For the wobbling: a discounted month for a subscriber not consistently engaging, a service-recovery code for a support issue, a renewal-date extension (capped twice a year, up to 90 days) for a delivery failure. Full portal self-service — status, tier moves, an easy off switch — is itself retention: easy-out is why refusers signed up at all, the promise [Get in](/growth-program-v3/get-in) made.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Here the page departs from its own sources, on purpose: the corpus stores every one of these levers at the cancellation screen, and even classifies skip/pause as a *warning signal* — the valve as symptom. The FIRST WEEKS framing inverts that: offered proactively, the valve is the medicine. A skip taken in week 4 is not a step toward the exit; it is the exit not taken. The mechanics are corpus-verified; the early, unprompted handover is an OpenSubs original — flagged as ours, argued from the pause-is-a-win accounting the platform layer already concedes.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The mesh

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The corpus's diagnostic for a steady bleed — churn constant over time rather than front-loaded — is exactly two missing things: no community ties and no engagement loop. The cure is structural: replace hub-and-spoke (every subscriber connected only to the brand) with a mesh, where they connect to each other.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Scaled to a CPG brand, the corpus's community playbook: at least one group event a month (its cadence ladder: every 21 days high, 42 moderate, 63 minimum), 2-3 manual member introductions a week, a monthly member spotlight, content categories that invite interaction (its four: Wins, Fun, Discovered, Meetups), the best new-member contributions pinned daily. Every event invites a guest — the built-in referral. The health metric is countable: how many members posted or commented in the last 7 days.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The corpus's own line is the whole argument: it is easy to quit a membership and hard to leave a relationship. A subscriber whose only tie is the box has one thread to cut; a subscriber in the mesh has many.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The milestone line

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The journey has four named milestones in order — Activate, Testimonial, Refer, Ascend — each with its unlock: the next thing opens at Activate, spotlight and badge at Testimonial, a free month or credit at Refer, VIP tier or founder pricing at Ascend. The lock-in behind the second: a public testimonial makes it psychologically harder to leave.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Run the line inside the first 90 days on the timing the corpus gives: the referral ask goes only to engaged openers and clickers — never blast it. The peer-to-peer referral pattern (subscriber shares an offer code, both sides benefit) is the CPG-native form. The handwritten card marks the early milestones — signup already sent one in First order; the next lands with the 3-month survival. And Ascend runs on the just-bought law: the moment of a milestone purchase is the moment to present the next offer, proactively, not on request.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Manufactured recognition converts a customer into someone with a public position to defend — a review posted, a friend referred, a badge held. Each milestone is a small identity investment, and identity investments do not churn casually.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Info>
**Frontier: milestones for physical goods.** The corpus's milestone cards are services-and-community artifacts — modules, spotlights, profile frames. "Shipment #3" and "your first 30 days on it" as celebrated moments for a CPG subscriber exist nowhere in the mined material. The translation ships as OpenSubs original work; the four-milestone spine above is the corpus-verified scaffold it hangs on.
</Info>

## The commitment pivot

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The corpus is blunt: the cliff is partly a monthly-billing artifact. Churn correlates directly with billing frequency — the worked table runs annual 2% monthly churn (\$5,000 LTV), quarterly 5% (\$2,000), monthly 10.7% (\$935): annual is 5.35x monthly LTV. Median net revenue retention runs 10-20 points higher on annual than monthly. And the timing law is the sharpest card in the deck: customers are 3-4x more likely to upgrade in month **two** than month nine.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Make the ask in month two, triggered on habit-formation behavior — the on-time second reorder, the never-skipped cadence — not on a calendar date. Sweeten it the corpus's way: credit past monthly spend toward the longer plan, and restrict that credit to the first six months (past the cliff you would be discounting people already staying). Keep the option visible somewhere prominent between asks. For the hesitant, the ladder from [Get bigger](/growth-program-v3/get-bigger) starts lower: quarterly first, or the corpus's short-duration play — sell 6-12 weeks up front, upsell the year after trust. Never force it: nudge and opt in.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Every billing event is a cancellation decision point; a subscriber who converts to quarterly in month two has three cliff-window decision points removed outright. The month-two window exists because habit formation peaks there — by month nine the subscriber has either committed behaviorally (and does not need the discount) or half-left (and no discount reaches them). The pivot does not defend the cliff. It deletes it.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The hands in this bucket

The early-warning system's most expensive tier is the hands: high-risk, high-value accounts get a human inside 24 hours, and some at-risk subscribers no model catches — the manual CRM override exists because a support agent's hunch is data. Service failures route to concierge recovery, never to a marketing flow. And the mesh runs on manual acts of hospitality — introductions, spotlights, the pinned first post. Patch what broke, then leave them with a little more than they asked for: in the cliff window, that second job is the difference between a subscriber who was processed and one who was kept.
