Grown-ups call it activation. On the track it is the stretch between two clicks — the one that started the subscription and the first scoop, pour, or application that makes it real. The window runs from order placed, through the wait, to the box landing, and it ends at one precise moment: the product has a physical place and a ritual in the customer's day. On the counter, not the back of the pantry.

[Get in](/growth-program-v3/get-in) ended at the click. [First weeks](/growth-program-v3/first-weeks) starts at order two. This bucket is everything in between, in five stages: the confirmation, the wait, the box, the ritual, and the machinery underneath.

## The figure

Three numbers define this window. First, the retention playbook's warning: expect churn to spike up to 50% in month one — dormant, half-decided subscribers surface and cancel the moment you start actually engaging them — then drop dramatically by months two and three (its worked trajectory: month one 10% to 15%, month two down to 7%, month three to 3%). The spike is not failure; it is the tree being shaken.

Second, the diagnosis: the same playbook maps high month-one-to-two churn to exactly one cause — no defined activation point and weak onboarding. Third, the stakes on speed: a low-tenure customer with an active complaint is 3-4x more likely to churn, so anything that goes wrong in this window gets answered before day 30 — not in next month's ticket sweep.

And the scoreboard for the whole bucket, one number: **the percentage of new subscribers who hit the activation point within 30 days.** If it is low, the playbook's prescription is blunt — make onboarding simpler, more personal, or more guided.

## The confirmation

The order exists; the relationship does not yet. Three surfaces fire in the first minute, and each one has craft.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Post-purchase messages are the best-read email your brand will ever send — the flow benchmarks run 50-65% open rates, because the recipient is actively awaiting order information. Whatever you put next to that information rides the highest attention you will ever get for free.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    The first email is thank-you plus what-happens-next, immediately. The lifecycle-email corpus gives it laws: name ONE primary next step, not a feature list. State the dates in plain text — what ships when, when the first renewal charges. Close with a P.S. that invites a reply (a reply channel opened on day zero is a complaint channel that skips the public review). Every lifecycle email carries a fallback plain URL for when the button fails. And make the promise the retention playbook demands: the subscriber always knows when they will hear from you next — first follow-up inside 24-48 hours.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The receipt is not a legal formality — the receipt template corpus treats it as three plays in one. A next-purchase coupon with an expiry date (a second sale riding a transactional open rate). The statement descriptor and card last-four named in plain text (chargeback insurance — the subscriber who recognizes the charge does not dispute it). And a proactive card-update prompt keyed to life events — moved recently, new card — which is the cheapest dunning email you will ever send, because it goes out before anything fails.
  </Step>
</Steps>

One more capture belongs at the sale, because it never gets easier than now: the second payment method. The pricing playbook's basis — roughly 1.2-1.7% of subscribers a month are lost to card changes alone, 24-34% of all churn at a 5% total rate — and its worked math puts a backup card at +31% to +51% LTV. [The crack](/growth-program-v3/the-crack) teaches the recovery machinery; this bucket's job is only the capture, at checkout or in the day-zero email, framed as never-miss-a-box.

<Info>
**Frontier: the thank-you page.** The screen one second after payment is the highest-attention surface in the whole lifecycle — and across all 773 mined cards there is not one about it. The corpus wrote the pre-purchase page a dozen laws and the post-purchase screen zero. What transfers: the one-next-step law from the welcome email, and the five-question skeleton from [Get in](/growth-program-v3/get-in). What OpenSubs is writing first, from scratch: the thank-you page as activation surface — what arrives when, the one thing to do before the box lands, and the reply channel. Original territory, under development; no borrowed numbers to lean on, and none faked.
</Info>

## The wait

Between the charge and the doorbell sits the doubt window. The card was charged; nothing has arrived; every hour of silence is space for buyer's remorse to work. The corpus's instruments for this stretch are consciousness and consent.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Multi-channel onboarding journeys hit 70%-plus average activation rates in the corpus's benchmark — against the single-channel default. The prescription that pairs with it: run onboarding on two channels simultaneously, in-product and email. For a CPG brand mid-wait, in-product means the account portal and the package tracking surface.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    An automated onboarding campaign runs through the wait to keep the product in consciousness — not marketing volume, but the notification doctrine the billing corpus states in three words: timely, purposeful, meaningful. Ship-confirmation, in-transit, arriving-today. Each is expected, each is opened, and each carries one line of ritual priming — what to do with box one, where it should live. Consent is the gate: marketing pushes and texts require explicit prior opt-in with a clear opt-out, so the opt-in is collected at checkout, not assumed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    If something goes wrong in this window — a complaint, a where-is-it, a charge dispute — the 3-4x churn multiplier is already running. The first human touch follows the retention playbook's ACA script: acknowledge what you see them doing, compliment something specific, ask a question. And the channel ladder from the same playbook holds: a call or a voice note saves more than an automated email, in exact proportion to the personal cost of sending it.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Info>
**Frontier: the doubt-window text.** Seventeen SMS mentions in the corpus, and every one fires at cart abandonment or win-back — not one card describes a text to a new subscriber in the first-order window. No copy, no timing, no shipping-notification craft. The consent gate above is real corpus law; the message itself is original territory. Same for **reinforcement ads at new subscribers**: every paid-media card in the corpus points outward (lookalikes) or backward (win-back) — nobody buys media against the person who just subscribed. The nearest transferable skeleton is the win-back display pattern (three creative phases, frequency-capped, on a defined audience). Both plays are under development as OpenSubs originals — named here so you know the territory exists, not dressed in numbers that do not.
</Info>

## The box

The clock this bucket runs on starts at the doorbell, not the charge. The email playbook is explicit about the trigger: delivery-timed sends run off Fulfilled Order, and the sequence is timed to the box — usage tips 2-3 days *after delivery*, the review ask 7-10 days *after delivery*. A tips email that lands before the product does is noise; the same email two days after unboxing is service.

Delivery itself is a churn variable, not a logistics footnote: warehouse-to-home distance shows up as a top-5 churn driver in the corpus's model. And when the shipment breaks, the machinery has laws: an out-of-stock installment is rescheduled, never cancelled, on a policy explicitly faster and gentler than card-failure handling — and the make-good for a real delivery failure is a renewal-date extension, pushing the next charge out so the subscriber never pays for the gap.

One physical artifact appears in the entire corpus: the genuinely handwritten card, sent at signup — two or three sentences, one personal detail. In a channel this empty, an object with handwriting on it is the loudest signal a CPG brand can ship.

<Info>
**Frontier: the object in the house.** Sweep 773 cards for unboxing, packaging, inserts, QR codes, placement — zero relevant hits. The corpus contains no card about a physical object arriving at a home. The insert that shows the ritual, the QR that plays the 60-second how-to, the packaging line that says where the tub lives — none of it has an evidentiary base anywhere in the mined material. This is the deepest gap in the deck and the biggest original-contribution opportunity in the program. OpenSubs is writing it first, from CPG first principles; it ships as its own play when it is real.
</Info>

## The ritual

The bucket's finish line. Everything above delivers a box; this stage makes the box a habit.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The retention playbook's definition, verbatim territory: for a physical product, the activation point is **the first time they use the consumable**. Its template: every customer that does X or gets Y stays longer than customers who do not. The stakes, from the subscription-services corpus: poor product adoption is unsubscribe reason number one. Non-activation is not a soft metric — it is the named churn cause.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Find your X from your own base, the playbook's six-step way: pull churned-customer data, filter for who stayed 3+ months, take the top 20% by spend, learn everything about them, find what they did differently in week one, and test the five best candidates. Then drive everyone to it: the routine-builder pattern from the corpus's skincare segment analysis — usage-education emails on how to use what they bought, paired with a short-dated incentive (its spec: a 10% coupon valid 14 days, the deadline being the point). Re-derive the activation criteria every 6-12 months, because it drifts.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The onboarding-format hierarchy explains why most CPG onboarding underperforms — every step down the ladder sheds effect: Custom beats Generic, Personal beats Group, Live beats Recorded, Carrots beat Sticks, Some beats None. A generic recorded drip is four rungs down. You do not need webinars to climb: a segment-cut email keyed to what they actually bought is Custom; a voice note from a founder is Personal and nearly Live. The corpus's six activation incentives (gated unlocks, earned calls, tier access, status markers) all reduce to carrots on the first use.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Two asks belong inside this stage because the psychology is time-boxed. First: customers who have just bought are the **least** likely to churn — so the playbook's ascension rule is to present the next offer proactively now, not to wait to be asked; the add-on or upgrade pitch rides the activation high, and waiting for "settled" wastes it. Second: the public testimonial, asked at the delivery+7-10-day review beat, is a lock-in device — the corpus is plain that a public endorsement makes it psychologically harder to leave. And one expectation gets set here that pays two buckets later: tell them during onboarding that if they ever want to leave, there is a quick exit conversation first — pre-framed now, it reads as process when [Jumped out](/growth-program-v3/jumped-out) needs it.

## The machinery

The plumbing that makes the four stages run without a human watching:

- **The scoreboard.** One number grades this bucket: % of new subscribers hitting the activation point within 30 days. The flow benchmarks underneath it: 50-65% open, 5-12% click, 5-15% review collection, 8-20% repeat purchase from the post-purchase flow.
- **The split.** The flow branches first-time versus repeat buyer at entry — a repeat buyer gets loyalty acknowledged and brand education skipped. First-order treatment is for first orders.
- **Flow hygiene.** Buyers auto-exit prospect flows (the has-not-ordered-since-entering filter). Automated sends respect a 16-hour minimum gap. Do not overload — the corpus's own ceiling law.
- **Personalization.** Real account context only — name, milestone, what they bought. Broken personalization is worse than none; "Hey there, %name!" is the corpus's own cautionary artifact.
- **Coupons.** Unique per-subscriber codes generated by the store integration, never static codes that leak to coupon sites.
- **The handoff.** Buyer behavior history moves from acquisition to retention at the moment of signup — what they browsed, which claim converted them — so the ritual emails speak to the person, not the segment.
- **The order that never became a customer.** Billing corpus: a subscription with an immediate payment due sits in an incomplete state, and on automatic collection there is a 23-hour window to land the first invoice before it expires. Watch that state — it is the only churn that happens before the relationship starts.
