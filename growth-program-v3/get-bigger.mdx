Grown-ups call it expansion. On the track it is the growth run — the last stretch, where the customer who stayed gets bigger as they roll. Two levers, and only two: fewer chances to quit, and more in the box. Every play on this page pulls one of them.

This bucket exists because kept subscribers are not a fixed asset. The cohort analysis behind [First weeks](/growth-program-v3/first-weeks) watched revenue retained climb from 100% at month zero to 278% by month 35 — the survivors nearly tripled their value. That growth is not automatic. It is fed. This page is the feeding.

## The figure

The corpus's LTV-lever benchmarks, so every play below has a denominator: a quality upsell converts 15-25% of those offered; a cross-sell 10-20%; a quantity upsell lifts cash collected 20%-plus; a downsell catches 30-50% of those about to take nothing. Stacked, the levers multiply — the corpus's worked example compounds a \$100/80%-margin product from \$80 of lifetime gross profit to \$378, a 4.7x, by stacking price, cross-sell, recurrence, and one upsell tier. You will not run all of them at once. Sequence by speed-to-impact, the corpus's own priority order: recurrence and churn first, then one upsell, then one downsell, then the cross-sell.

## Fewer chances to quit: stretch the billing clock

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Every billing event is a cancellation decision point. The pricing playbook's interval ladder from [Get in](/growth-program-v3/get-in) works this bucket harder: quarterly billing turns twelve yes-again moments a year into four — and \$300-per-12-weeks fits a fifth billing cycle inside the year that \$100-monthly cannot (+25% annualized on the same subscriber).
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Offer the quarterly or prepaid plan at the moments a subscriber has just proven the habit: after the third box, inside the portal, and in the winback reactivation surface. The corpus's ladder frame does the selling — quote the standard price first, then the prepaid option at roughly 8% off reads as an earned concession, not a commitment trap. Never lead with the long plan on a new subscriber; commitment is bought with evidence, and evidence takes three boxes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The interval is the churn lever wearing a pricing costume. Fewer renewals means fewer moments where quitting is one click away — and the prepaid discount costs less than the churn it prevents at every rung of the corpus's own math.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## More in the box: the quantity ladder

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Three quantity moves in the corpus, one mechanism: bulk (more units, or prepay a longer period), more often (shorter interval), bigger (larger size per unit). Anchoring the larger option first lifts cash collected 20%-plus, with the standard size as the downsell.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    In CPG the natural upgrade beats is the portal and the pre-renewal email: offer the larger size or the add-a-unit before the next box locks. Households that burn through faster get the shorter interval offered when their skip history says never-skips. The downsell runs the same ladder in reverse — the subscriber about to cancel over volume gets the smaller box or longer interval, which is a retention play wearing an expansion costume.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Quantity grows revenue with zero acquisition cost and — for bulk and bigger — fewer shipments per dollar, so margin grows faster than revenue. The anchor-then-concede structure works on subscribers for the same reason it worked on prospects in Get in: the standard option reads as the reasonable middle once a larger one exists.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## More in the box: the cross-sell

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    Corpus benchmark: 10-20% of the base takes a well-chosen cross-sell, and added LTV = take rate × cross-sell gross profit — both factors matter, so a high-margin add-on at 10% beats a thin one at 20%.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    The corpus's design rules, CPG-translated: serve the same customer (the person already opening your box), solve the next problem (the product they would buy after this one — the conditioner to the shampoo, the filter to the machine), keep operations simple (no new supplier for a 12% take), and do not break the business for extra change. Two surfaces: the one-time add-on to next month's box (no new commitment, lowest friction), and the second-line subscription for proven takers.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The box is a distribution channel you already paid for. An add-on rides existing pick-and-pack at near-zero marginal fulfillment cost, and each additional product line in the box is one more reason the whole subscription survives the next budget review.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Grow the person, not just the order

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The tier analysis from [Jumped out](/growth-program-v3/jumped-out), read from the other end: top-tier subscribers held over 2x the LTV of basic-tier (\$123 vs \$52) with the lowest churn of any tier. Tier position is not just a revenue line — it predicts survival.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Build the visible next rung: the member tier, the bigger bundle, the early-access shelf for subscriber-only products. Offer the upgrade on evidence — tenure, add-on history, never-skips — through the portal and the post-box-three email, the same peak-trust moments as the billing stretch. Testimonials from long-tenure subscribers (the stock [Get in](/growth-program-v3/get-in) told you to collect) are the proof layer here: the upgrade pitch is another subscriber saying the bigger box was worth it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    Selection and treatment both run through tiers: the subscriber who upgrades is telling you they intend to stay, and the richer tier gives them more reasons to keep meaning it. That is the two levers pulled at once — more in the box, and fewer moments where leaving feels cheap.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The hands in this bucket

Expansion is where support's second job — leave them with a little more than they asked for — becomes the revenue line. The ticket about a late box ends with the shipment fixed and a mention that the two-bag size exists. The how-do-I-use-this conversation ends with the add-on that answers it. No scripts, no quotas: the corpus's cross-sell rules already define the boundary — same customer, next problem. A support team that knows the catalog is a sales team the subscriber thanked.
