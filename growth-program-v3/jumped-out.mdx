Grown-ups call it voluntary churn. On the track it is the ball that bounced out the side — nothing broke, nobody fumbled a card. A person weighed the box against the money and chose out. That choice is information, and this bucket is about collecting it, answering it, and — for the leavers worth it — reversing it in a conversation.

## The figure

Split your monthly leavers into chose-to-go and card-broke before you fix anything, because the two need opposite medicine — this bucket runs on persuasion, the next one runs on machines. The map's split: of every 7 subscribers who leave in a month, 3 never chose to — which makes the choosers the other 4, the majority, and the ones this page is for.

Then look at *who* chooses. In a subscription dataset's churn analysis, the cheapest tier churned at 13.78% — the worst of every tier — at \$52 lifetime value against \$123 up-tier. And look at *when*: your platform's cancellation reporting shows days-from-start-to-cancel, and that distribution tells you whether people quit before the habit (a first-weeks problem) or out of a habit gone stale (this bucket's problem).

## Ask at the door

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    You cannot answer a reason you never collected. The offboarding pattern the corpus holds up as the standard: a cancellation form offering up to three reasons from a fixed menu, plus one open question — what could we have done better — with no guilt trip anywhere in the flow.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Put a one-click reason selector inside the cancel flow itself — not in an email afterward, when response rates collapse. Fixed menu, three selections max, one open text field. Then read it weekly as a ranked list: the top two reasons are your roadmap, and everything below them is noise until the top two move.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The reasons cluster into answerable and unanswerable. Too much product, too expensive right now, wanted variety — answerable with the alternatives below. Moved away, allergic, doctor said stop — unanswerable, and knowing their share tells you the ceiling on every save play you will ever run.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Offer the smaller yes before the exit

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The winback playbook's cancel-flow spec leads with alternatives, in order: pause, reduce frequency, swap product — before any discount is mentioned.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Wherever your platform keeps its cancellation flow, the sequence is: reason first, then the alternative matched to the reason. Too much product gets a skip or a longer interval. Needs a break gets pause. Bored gets a swap. Price gets — last, and only if you choose to play it — an offer. A pause is not a lost month; it is a subscriber who did not cancel.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    A large share of cancel intent in CPG is really inventory intent — the shelf is full. Overwhelm is the corpus's number one stated churn driver, and no discount fixes a full shelf. The skip does. Matching the alternative to the stated reason is what makes the flow feel like service instead of a maze of retention tricks — and the trial promise from Get in (one-click cancel, no traps) is a promise this flow has to keep.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The exit conversation, for the leavers worth it

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The retention playbook's exit-interview math: about half of leavers will take the conversation, and about half of those are saved — cutting churn roughly 25% and lifting LTV about a third. Its channel ranking, by save rate: phone call first, then voice or video message, then a direct text or DM, then personal email, with automated email last — save rate rises with the perceived personal cost of the channel.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Do not run this on everyone — run it on the leavers whose LTV pays for a human's time: long tenure, high spend, multi-product. The expectation was set back in the first weeks (if you ever leave, we talk first), so the invitation reads as process, not desperation. In the conversation: hear the reason, fix what is fixable on the spot, and take the no gracefully when it is a real no.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    A cancellation is usually an unresolved complaint wearing a cancel button. The conversation is the only place the complaint can actually be resolved — a coupon just postpones it one cycle and teaches the subscriber that leaving is how you get deals.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Catch the wobble before the jump

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The corpus's predictive-intervention pattern: define an engagement threshold, alert when an account drops below it, run a standard intervention, and track the save rate — plus alerts on downgrade attempts specifically.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    In CPG the wobble is visible in the portal: a skip, a frequency stretch, a visit to the cancel page that did not finish. Treat two skips in a row as a raised hand. The intervention is not a panicked discount — it is the same alternatives menu, offered early, plus a human touch for high-value accounts before the decision hardens.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    By the time the cancel flow runs, the decision is mostly made and you are negotiating at the door. The skip data says the same thing two cycles earlier, when the answer is still cheap.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The hands in this bucket

The save is a conversation, not a coupon — which makes this bucket support's home turf. The machines run the reason menu and the alternatives; the humans run the exit interviews, in the channel order the corpus ranked (a phone call saves more than an email precisely because it costs you more). And the second support job — leave them with a little more than they asked for — applies even at the exit: the leaver who was handled generously at the door is the easiest winback on the list.

The border of this bucket is choice. Everything here assumed a person deciding. The other kind of leaver decided nothing — the card just died, and no persuasion in the world fixes a card. Machines fix cards: [The crack](/growth-program-v3/the-crack).
