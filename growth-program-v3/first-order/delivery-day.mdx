The box is the product's first physical argument for itself, and everything in this chapter obeys one clock rule: delivery starts the clock, not the charge. A tips email timed to the order date lands before the product does — noise. The same email two days after unboxing is service.

## The clock

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The email playbook is explicit about the trigger: delivery-timed sends run off Fulfilled Order, not Placed Order — and the sequence is timed to the box: usage tips 2-3 days *after delivery*, the review ask 7-10 days *after delivery*.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Rebuild the post-purchase flow on the fulfillment trigger wherever a send references the physical product. Thank-you and receipt ride the charge (previous chapter); tips, how-to-use, and the review ask ride the doorbell. If your ESP only knows the order date, you are mailing a schedule, not a customer.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The sequence order is load-bearing: usage help arrives before the review request so the subscriber has actually experienced the product when asked to grade it. Every beat timed to delivery removes a distinct early-quit cause — confusion at the box, silence after it.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The box as a churn variable

Delivery is not a logistics footnote. Warehouse-to-home distance shows up as a top-5 churn driver in the corpus's model — the subscriber two zones out lives with slower boxes and looser arrival windows, and their churn rate says so. You cannot move the warehouse per subscriber, but you can time expectations honestly by zone, and the corpus offers a discount-substitute worth testing: a free shipping upgrade (Standard to Express) A/B tested against a price discount — the same money spent on speed instead of margin theater.

When the shipment itself breaks, the machinery has laws: a scheduled shipment can fail three distinct ways, each with its own handler; an out-of-stock installment is **rescheduled, never cancelled**, on a policy explicitly faster and gentler than card-failure handling; and the make-good for a real delivery failure is a renewal-date extension — push the next charge out (the billing corpus caps it at twice a year, up to 90 days each) so the subscriber never pays for the gap.

## The one physical artifact the corpus knows

One physical object appears in the entire mined corpus: the genuinely handwritten card — sent at signup, two or three sentences, one personal detail. In a channel this empty, an object with handwriting on it is the loudest signal a CPG brand can ship, and box one is its natural envelope.
