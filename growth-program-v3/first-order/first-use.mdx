The bucket's finish line. Everything before this delivered a box; this chapter makes the box a habit. The window ends when the product has a physical place and a ritual in the customer's day — on the counter, not the back of the pantry.

## The definition

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The retention playbook's definition, verbatim territory: for a physical product, the activation point is **the first time they use the consumable**. Its template: every customer that does X or gets Y stays longer than customers who do not. The stakes, from the subscription-services corpus: poor product adoption is unsubscribe reason number one. Non-activation is not a soft metric — it is the named churn cause.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Find your X from your own base, the playbook's six-step way: pull churned-customer data, filter for who stayed 3+ months, take the top 20% by spend, learn everything about them, find what they did differently in week one, and test the five best candidates. Re-derive every 6-12 months, because activation criteria drift as the product and the base change.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    A subscriber who never reaches first value has no reason to be there when box two charges. Every play in this chapter drives one number — defined in [the flows chapter](/growth-program-v3/first-order/flows-underneath) as the bucket's scoreboard.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Drive to the first use

<Steps>
  <Step title="The figure">
    The corpus's worked pattern for consumables is the routine builder: usage-education content on how to use exactly what they bought, paired with a short-dated incentive — its spec, from a skincare segment analysis: a 10% coupon valid 14 days, the deadline being the point.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fix">
    Run the retention playbook's six-step onboarding sequence, CPG-sized: outline how to get value (the one first use, shown), resell the purchase (why they said yes, restated), show what unlocks as they stay, teach how to reach you, follow up on the known schedule, and connect them to other customers. The playbook's commitment move translates too: tell them what to stop buying — the retail habit this subscription replaces — and the first use gets a hole in the routine to land in.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The why">
    The onboarding-format hierarchy explains why most CPG onboarding underperforms — every step down sheds effect: Custom beats Generic, Personal beats Group, Live beats Recorded, Carrots beat Sticks, Some beats None. A generic recorded drip is four rungs down. You do not need webinars to climb: a segment-cut email keyed to what they actually bought is Custom; a voice note from a founder is Personal and nearly Live. The corpus's six activation incentives (gated unlocks, earned calls, tier access, status markers, and the rest) all reduce to carrots on the first use.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The asks that ride the activation high

Two asks belong inside this stage because the psychology is time-boxed. First: customers who have just bought are the **least** likely to churn — so the playbook's ascension rule is to present the next offer proactively now, not to wait to be asked; the add-on or upgrade pitch rides the high, and waiting for "settled" wastes it. Second: the public testimonial, asked at the delivery+7-10-day review beat, is a lock-in device — the corpus is plain that a public endorsement makes it psychologically harder to leave.

And one expectation gets set here that pays two buckets later: tell them during onboarding that if they ever want to leave, there is a quick exit conversation first — pre-framed now, it reads as process when [Jumped out](/growth-program-v3/jumped-out) needs it.
